# Dictionary audit decisions

The user's rulings of 2026-10-28 left the following calls to the auditor. Each entry names the ruling it applies, the decision, and the keys it affects, so a later review can revisit one without re-deriving it. The rulings themselves are in the dictionary-audit skill's sources reference, under "Editorial rulings".

## Rulings applied

- **Faithfulness to the print decides structure.** The application's routing, lookup and layout never weigh against a restoration. Where faithful structure needs application work, the application changes.
- **Pronunciations** are restored from the print only inside entries a batch already reviews.
- **Every 1913 entry gets a record.**
- **Senses follow the print's division and numbering.**
- **Numbered homograph references** ("See 3d Sore") link to that homograph, and reviews confirm the record's homograph numbering against the print (2026-11-02).
- **Print against the typed text (2026-11-10).** Where two certain readings agree on a printed word and the 1898 printing reads the same, the printed word replaces the electronic one. This covers a word that differs, a word or article the record lacks inside a sentence it carries, and a word the record adds. A letter damaged in both printings, or a printed error a 1913 reference corrects, leaves the record as it is. Whole sentences, examples, quotations and notes found only in the print stay out.
- **Held fields ruled on 2026-11-02.** A shared word that differs from the clear print only in confusable letters is wrong when its context makes the printed word evident. A shared citation stays as printed when only an inference about the etymology contradicts it. A visible accent takes LSJ's form when LSJ attests one placement with a matching gloss. A heading whose primary stress falls on a syllable its own pronunciation marks secondary takes the printed stress. Two senses with the same number are renumbered. A printed sign with no exact Unicode character takes the nearest agreed character.
- **Held fields ruled on 2026-11-03.** A heading takes the printed heading, ‖ included, when its printed pronunciation divides or stresses the word that way (Schizocoele becomes ‖ Schizˊo-cœ′le). Pape and Bailly supply a breathing LSJ lacks but never an accent, so Sciomachy keeps σκιομαχια. "G." before a Greek word is the German label set by mistake and becomes "Gr." (Bolus, Bosporian, Cochineal, Copperas, Croesus, with Rupia in its batch). Roe's etymology restores the printed "Dan. raa", lost by an eye-skip between rā and raa, and the rose beetle's sense (b) restores the printed "rose chafer", which the record's own Chafer and Rose chafer entries require; both apply with batch z35. For a Greek genitive LSJ gives only in its entry heading, the heading's ending counts as LSJ's form, so Ripidolite's ῥιπῖδος becomes ῥιπίδος (an auditor call the user delegated).
- **Supplement entries the 1907 printing lacks** take Webster's New International Dictionary as their print witness. Its text is accepted only where it matches Gutenberg word for word, and a pronunciation mark the corpus never uses is held.

## Decisions

### Smack

### Critical temperature (merge P15)

The print has the compound "Critical point (Physics)" under Critical and no "Critical temperature". The record's Critical temperature is a later rewording of that compound. Critical regains the printed compound, and the reworded record folds into it, so the separate key goes. Applied.

### Compounds in their own records

188 keys, such as Cabbage butterfly, Humming bird and Sodium bicarbonate, hold a compound that the print runs in under its head word. Each merges into its head entry in the printed position, read from the page. These keys were left alone earlier because of how our records are organized, a reason the faithfulness ruling removes.

The merge goes in two steps. Where the head entry already prints the compound and the own record adds no word to it, the own record is a duplicate and is removed (16 records, such as Anchor watch, Blueprint and Trustee process, none of which Gutenberg prints as a separate entry). Where the own record carries text the print lacks, the head entry receives the printed compound and the own record stays as a later-addition entry, because the rulings keep text only GCIDE has; 109 of these 122 records are classified in the later-additions registry, and the other 13 consist mostly of printed text. A pilot of 10 compounds missing from their head entries is read twice blind before its own records are removed.

### Notes and repeated senses in a rewritten run

A note the print lacks entirely moves after the printed senses, so it no longer interrupts them, as Fermentation's industrial-microbiology note does. A record sense whose words the print already sets inside one of its senses is dropped rather than kept as a later addition, as with Canteen's "The sutler's shop in a garrison."

### Reworded senses

A record sense that rewords a printed sense, such as Galaxy's "splendid or impressive" sense against the print's "A splendid assemblage of persons or things", counts as that printed sense, so the printed wording replaces it rather than standing beside it as a later addition. Only a sense whose meaning the print lacks entirely is kept after the printed senses.

### Which pronunciations a batch restores

A batch restores the headword's own pronunciation when the print has one and the record's is missing or partial. Pronunciations inside inflection lists ("Occurred", "Smacked") and plural placeholders such as `(#)` stay for a later pass over those lists, because each needs its own target and crop.

### Later senses after printed ones

When the print divides or numbers senses differently, the record follows the print. A sense that only GCIDE has follows the printed senses and takes the next number. The later-additions registry keeps classifying it.

### Printed errors in new records

Globard's etymology loses its closing bracket at a line end in the print, and Gutenberg prints it, so the record restores it. Estacade's pronunciation opens with a u under a ticked macron in every printing checked, which cannot be the French "es", and Calamary's "-mū̍-ry̆" has the same glyph where the word has an a. Both records keep the printed glyph, and holds ask for a witness to the intended mark.

### Stress where entry readers split

Where two readers of a new entry differ on a headword stress mark, Gutenberg's headword notation decides it. Generatrix takes `Genˊer-a′trix` from Gutenberg's ``Gen`er*a"trix``, and Hiphalt takes a secondary final mark. A stress mark that both readers agree on stands even where Gutenberg differs, as with Gunsmith and Jailer.

### Main entries beside cross-reference records

Semeiotics and Sloo are printed main entries ("Semeiotics, or Semiotics, n. Semeiology"; "Sloo, or Slue, n. A slough"). The records Semiotics and Slue hold the print's separate cross-reference entries for the variant spellings. Each main entry gets its own record, and the cross-reference records stay.

Torpedo loses the Farragut quotation and the "marine mine" and "land mine" remarks, and Installation loses its Baton Rouge example. ### New International witnesses

Flatware and Rossel current are absent from the only scanned printing of the 1900 supplement, so Webster's New International Dictionary witnesses them. Its house style differs from the 1913 supplement's: it lowercases headwords, gives each word of an open compound a primary accent, and abbreviates field labels without parentheses. The records take their headword notation and field label from Gutenberg's transcription of the 1913 supplement, and their pronunciations from the New International print. Rossel current is therefore `Ros′sel curˊrent` with the label "(Oceanography)", where the New International prints two primary accents and "Oceanog.".

### Printed errors a 1913 reference corrects

Soak cites "Isa. xxiv. 7." for "Their land shall be soaked with blood" in the print, GCIDE and Gutenberg alike. In the Authorized Version that line is Isaiah 34:7, and 24:7 is "The new wine mourneth", so the record reads "Isa. xxxiv. 7.". Solar's table of periodic comets prints Halley's last perihelion as 1885.9. The Encyclopaedia Britannica of 1910–11 gives 1835 Nov. 15.9, and the table's own period of 76 years leads from 1835 to the 1910 return, so the record reads 1835.9. Each correction names its reference in the batch evidence.

### Eye-skips inside words

An eye-skip can start and end inside words. Slime's etymology prints "MHG. slīm, OHG. slīmen", and the electronic "MHG. slīmen" lacks exactly the span between the two "slīm"s. Its second sense prints "mucilaginous or glutinous", and the electronic text lacks the span between the two "-inous" endings. A span that falls between two identical letter strings is lost the same way as one between repeated words, so both are restored as transcription loss.

### Later homographs after printed ones

Where a record carries homographs the print lacks, such as GCIDE-only or 1913 supplement homographs, they follow the printed homographs in the printed order, as later senses follow printed senses. Tout keeps its four printed homographs first and its later additions after them, so numbered references count the printed homographs.

### Duplicate sense numbers

Slip's noun ends its printed senses at 20. GCIDE adds a commented-out 21 for a slip dock and three later senses numbered 22, 23 and 23, and Gutenberg prints none of them, so no witness numbers them. The Marine Insurance sense becomes 24 and gains the id 3-24. Senses 22 and 23 keep their numbers and ids, because the excluded slip-dock sense explains the gap at 21 and the source rules keep an inherited number in that case. Its later-additions span follows the new number.

### Voiced th

The print sets a voiced th as a tied ligature, and GCIDE writes it `&thlig_;`, but the import flattened it to a plain th. Records now write it `t&#x35F;h`. A dictionary-wide pass compared each headword and inflected-form pronunciation with GCIDE's and marked 119 voiced th in 118 fields of 85 records, inserting the mark only where the letters, digits and count of th agreed exactly. Where homographs share their letters but differ in voicing, GCIDE's part of speech chose the field: Mouth, Path and Loathly keep a voiceless noun or adverb beside a voiced verb or adjective. Smeeth's second homograph is marked from the print, because GCIDE gives no pronunciation. Its crossbar runs unbroken into the h, unlike its first homograph's, and a blind reader saw the same. Pronunciations restored from print before this rule, and the 66,000 that remain untranscribed, keep a plain th until a batch reviews them.
